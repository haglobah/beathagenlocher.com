There's an interesting property of systems that rarely gets talked about:

Often, _limiting your options actually increases your capability to do stuff_ (≙ your power).

This is very, very opposed to a thing I regularly _see_ people doing: Just adding options to doing something.

I'm not sure how intuitive this is, but it is a thing I've seen people get wrong, so let's talk about it.

---

The thing that originally made me think about this is the trajectory of features programming languages have.

The features most used in mainstream programming languages roughly transformed from 'everything is possible' (machine code) to restricted access to registers and jumps, but with access to pointers; (C), to restricting access to pointers (e.g. Java) to restricting access to all mutation (functional programming).

(Yes, I know about the separate Lambda Calculus/Lisp/Haskell timeline, but I'm talking about mainstream—and those features only made it into the mainstream after Java happened.)

And the result of this restriction was that people nowadays are way, way more capable to write big and complex programs that have lots of functionality the people of the past only could dream about, and I think it's to a large because they reduced their leeway to do things on a different layer of the stack.[^1]

And with time, I've started to notice many more things that can be viewed through that lens.

---

And I went with that. But this is a thing that's happening in so many other different domains:

## 1. Things that work that way, and work well

- Language: Language only works because speakers limited themselves to a fixed set of sounds they use, and that let's both keep them in their head, and make them legible to others
  - and on a higher level, a <Il href="stream#00073">lingua franca</Il> is like that: We humans limit us to only one language we use to do almost everything in a particular topic
- currency is like that: instead of trading in any good, we trade for one, common, agreed-upon good.
  - Again on a higher level, a reserve currency is like that: By only limiting yourself to currency issued by a certain bank or country, everyone gets a medium of exchange to agree on, everyone benefits (and yes, the issuer of the reserve currency tends to benefit most)
- minimalism!
  - just owning very little makes _so_ _many_ things in life easier, it's hard to even describe
  - like not owning a car means just you need to never think about parking space, repairing it, going to a gas station, ...

## 1.5. Things that work that way, and don't work well (I think?) (aka. things where our options are already way too limited)

- zoning laws are the perfect example for this: Instead of allowing anyone to build anything anywhere, they prescribe certain areas where only some types of buildings can be built. (Is that sensible?)

## 2. Things where we should limit the options to acquire more power

- basically everything regarding regulation and law?
  here, my perception is that there's often way, way too much different things one could do and apply for, and that greatly limits the efficacy of the system in total—but that's often also the actual intent of the designers of the system or at least a desired side effect, so who knows
  - specifically the building code

# When to use this

I think the biggest personal usecase for this might be actually making decisions.
I know that I suffer a lot less from decision fatigue than people around me, and I also know people were confused when I told them about this.

So basically whenever something feels overwhelming, and I feel tempted to add an additional step/option/whatever to something (which has big negative externalities in complexity, but only in the long run, so it doesn't feel that way _right now_ when I'm making the decision), I take a step back and think whether I could actually achieve a better result by limiting my options (and maybe then improving my choices inside of those limited options).

Random examples that come to mind are:

- choosing things in new supermarkets (just go with only organic for first)
- buying clothing (pick the nicest fabric and ignore everything else)
- doing any of the next tasks on my todo list (there, I cheat and tackle them by prioritization)

I think that this is also a pretty nice general heuristic:

Often you don't even need to look at all of the options before deciding anything

Take language:
There, we effectively limit the amount of sound to a few handful
Did you notice?
All of that was just a different way to describe the process of abstraction—but this is something that's very hard to do like that.

The underlying principle behind this is that all of these are instances of a thing from software engineering: **Interfaces**

There, you consciously limit yourself to only using a very limited subset of all the available options at the benefit of simplicity—and I think this is what underlies this here:

> Simplicity is Power.
